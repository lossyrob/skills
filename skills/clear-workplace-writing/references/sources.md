# Sources and editorial decisions

`core-practices.md` is an original synthesis. The historical sources supply
principles, examples, cautions, and traceability. They are not a hierarchy of
rules.

## William Strunk Jr., *The Elements of Style*

- Edition: first edition, 1918.
- Bundled file: `elements-of-style-1918.md`.
- Address: `Strunk R1` through `Strunk R18`.
- Public-domain basis: published in the United States in 1918.
- Digital source used for this repository:
  [`obra/the-elements-of-style`](https://github.com/obra/the-elements-of-style),
  which packages the 1918 public-domain rules without later copyrighted
  additions. The introduction was verified against
  [Project Gutenberg eBook 37134](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/37134).

The bundled file contains Strunk's original introduction, Chapters II and III,
and the usage reference from Chapter V. It omits Chapter IV (matters of form),
Chapter VI (spelling), and Chapter VII (exercises), which are outside this
skill's source-specific review needs.

The synthesis qualifies several prescriptions. Active voice serves clarity of
agency rather than operating as an absolute. Positive form does not prohibit a
necessary negation. Economy does not justify removing evidence or caveats.

## Edwin A. Abbott, *How to Write Clearly*

- Edition: Fourth and Revised Edition, Roberts Brothers, Boston, 1883; first
  published in 1872.
- Bundled files: `abbott-index.md` and `abbott-rules.md`.
- Address: `Abbott R1` through `Abbott R56`; lettered rules retain their
  suffixes. Use `R10a-prime` for Abbott's printed `10 a'`.
- Public-domain basis: publication before 1929; Abbott died in 1926.
- Digital source:
  [Project Gutenberg eBook 22600](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/22600).

The bundled corpus contains Abbott's rule index and rules. It excludes the
preface, short exercises, continuous exercises, publisher advertisements, and
digital-edition boilerplate. Rule numbers are stable across the 1872 and 1883
editions.

The synthesis rejects Abbott's general preference for metaphor (R13 and R46),
does not adopt antithesis as a default source of force (R41), and treats his
that/which rule (R8) as historical rather than controlling usage.

## Herbert Spencer, *The Philosophy of Style*

- Primary print edition: *Essays: Scientific, Political, & Speculative*,
  Library Edition, Williams & Norgate, 1891, volume II, pages 333-369.
- Bundled file: `spencer-excerpts.md`.
- Address: `Spencer §N` follows the editorial section numbering in Project
  Gutenberg eBook 5849. `Spencer PS` identifies the Postscript preserved in
  eBook 53395.
- Public-domain basis: first published in 1852; Spencer died in 1903.
- Digital sources:
  [eBook 5849](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/5849) and
  [eBook 53395](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/53395).

The standalone eBook 5849 omits the Postscript, where Spencer qualifies his
preference for short, native words and defends precise abstract terminology.
The excerpts therefore use both editions.

The synthesis treats economy of attention as a useful editorial question, not
a literal theory of cognition. It rejects Spencer's Saxon-versus-Latinate
prescription, colonialist examples, and document-level preference for
qualifier-first periodic sentences. It preserves his cautions against constant
emphasis, excessive compression, and applying one structure everywhere.

## Modern workplace guidance

The synthesis also contains original guidance for preserving facts and
uncertainty, exposing causal reasoning, adapting technical detail to the
reader, and avoiding recurring model-generated writing habits. These practices
are labeled by their wording and rationale rather than assigned a false
historical lineage.

Other acknowledged intellectual influences include Joseph Williams, George
Gopen and Judith Swan, and Barbara Minto. Their works are not bundled or
reproduced.
