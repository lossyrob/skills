# Selected excerpts from Herbert Spencer's *The Philosophy of Style*

These public-domain excerpts preserve useful rationales and their limits.
`core-practices.md` remains the practical authority.

Section numbers follow the editorial numbering in the standalone digital
edition described in `sources.md`. The Postscript comes from the 1891 Library
Edition.

## Spencer §3 - Reader effort

> A reader or listener has at each moment but a limited amount of mental
> power available. To recognize and interpret the symbols presented to him,
> requires part of this power; to arrange and combine the images suggested
> requires a further part; and only that part which remains can be used for
> realizing the thought conveyed.

**Use:** Ask what decoding or reconstruction the writer can do for the reader.

**Qualification:** Treat this as an editorial metaphor. Some effort is useful,
especially when evidence or technical reasoning must remain inspectable.

## Spencer §5 - Familiar words

> The synonyms which [a child] learns in after years, never become so closely,
> so organically connected with the ideas signified, as do these original
> words used in childhood; and hence the association remains less strong.

> The earliest learnt and oftenest used words will, other things equal, call
> up images with less loss of time and energy than their later learnt
> synonyms.

**Use:** Prefer familiar words when they carry the same meaning.

**Qualification:** Familiarity and frequency matter; a word's Germanic or
Latinate origin does not. See the Postscript.

## Spencer §9-§10 - Specific and concrete language

> That concrete terms produce more vivid impressions than abstract ones, and
> should, when possible, be used instead, is a thorough maxim of composition.

> When an abstract word is used, the hearer or reader has to choose from his
> stock of images, one or more, by which he may figure to himself the genus
> mentioned.

**Use:** Prefer observable behavior, exact mechanisms, named components, and
examples when they make a claim easier to understand or test.

**Qualification:** Abstract terms are often the precise choice for abstract
ideas. See the Postscript.

## Spencer §24 - Keep related words together

> The words and expressions most nearly related in thought shall be brought
> the closest together.

> The longer the time that elapses between the mention of any qualifying
> member and the member qualified, the longer must the mind be exerted in
> carrying forward the qualifying member ready for use.

**Use:** Keep modifiers, clauses, and the words they govern close together.

**Qualification:** Front-loading every qualifier creates a different burden.
See §27, §30, and §32.

## Spencer §27 - Know when to stop

> Though, up to a certain point, it is well for the qualifying clauses of a
> period to precede those qualified; yet, as carrying forward each qualifying
> clause costs some mental effort, it follows that when the number of them and
> the time they are carried become great, we reach a limit beyond which more
> is lost than is gained.

**Use:** Stop applying a sound rule when another important quality begins to
degrade.

**Qualification:** The practical authority defaults to the governing point
early. This excerpt limits Spencer's qualifier-first argument rather than
establishing it as a workplace-writing rule.

## Spencer §28 - Consider the reader

> This question may sometimes be decided by considering the capacity of the
> persons addressed.

**Use:** Adapt structure and detail to the reader's knowledge and task.

**Qualification:** Spencer's surrounding language ranks readers
condescendingly. The useful principle is audience adaptation.

## Spencer §30-§32 - Use an intermediate sentence structure

> There are many cases, however, in which neither the direct nor the indirect
> structure is the best; but where an intermediate structure is preferable to
> both.

> Even when addressing the most vigorous intellects, the direct style is
> unfit for communicating ideas of a complex or abstract character.

**Use:** Introduce the main clause before a long stack of qualifications, then
attach remaining detail where it is easiest to apply.

**Qualification:** Spencer calls qualifier-first periodic sentences "direct."
That terminology is the reverse of common modern workplace usage and should
not be imported.

## Spencer §43 - Admit only useful metaphors

> A limit is put to the advantageous use of the Metaphor, by the condition
> that it must be sufficiently simple to be understood from a hint.
> Evidently, if there be any obscurity in the meaning or application of it, no
> economy of attention will be gained; but rather the reverse.

**Use:** Keep a metaphor only when it is accurate and easier to grasp than the
literal explanation.

## Spencer §47 - Select details that carry the rest

> To select from the sentiment, scene, or event described those typical
> elements which carry many others along with them; and so, by saying a few
> things but suggesting many, to abridge the description; is the secret of
> producing a vivid impression.

**Use:** Choose examples and evidence that establish the larger point without
cataloging every available detail.

**Qualification:** Do not omit evidence, caveats, or definitions the reader
needs to trust the conclusion.

## Spencer §64-§65 - Do not sustain maximum emphasis

> It warns us against the error committed both by Pope in his poems and by
> Bacon in his essays--the error, namely, of constantly employing forcible
> forms of expression ... the most perfectly-constructed sentences will soon
> weary.

> We should not make every section of our subject progress in interest; we
> should not always rise to a climax.

> We must subordinate the component effect to the total effect.

**Use:** Reserve emphasis for the parts that deserve it. This is the rationale
for avoiding unsupported intensifiers, dramatic transitions, and repeated
conclusions.

**Qualification:** Spencer's accompanying physiology is obsolete; the
editorial observation stands without it.

## Spencer §66-§67 - Vary form with the material

> Constant variety in the mode of presenting ideas ... will in a great degree
> result from a skilful adaptation of the form to the matter.

> To have a specific style is to be poor in speech.

**Use:** Match structure, rhythm, and formality to the material rather than
imposing one house voice on every document.

**Qualification:** A recognizable voice is not a defect. Variety should serve
the material and reader, not become a goal by itself.

## Spencer PS - Qualifications on brevity and abstraction

> The conclusion that because of their comparative brevity and because of
> those stronger associations formed by more frequent use, words of
> Old-English origin are preferable to words derived from Latin or Greek,
> should be taken with two qualifications.

> We have no word of native origin which can be advantageously substituted for
> the word "grand." ... In this case, then, a word of Latin origin is better
> than its nearest equivalent of native origin, because by use it has acquired
> an additional meaning.

> The greater brevity of a word does not invariably conduce to greater force.
> Where the word ... is one conveying the central element of the idea ... a
> longer word is sometimes better than a shorter word.

> The second qualification ... concerns the superiority of words derived from
> Latin or Greek, in cases where more or less abstract ideas have to be
> expressed. In such cases it is undesirable to use words having concrete
> associations.

> Such an expression as "the colligation of facts" is better for philosophical
> purposes than such an expression as "the tying together of facts."

**Use:** Choose words for meaning, familiarity, precision, and appropriate
emphasis. Word length, etymology, and concreteness are never sufficient tests.
